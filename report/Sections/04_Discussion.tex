\section{Discussion}

Under the subject-disjoint protocol the Mamba classifier edges out the
baselines on both feet -- two accuracy points and $0.03$ AUC over a tuned
Random Forest -- but it loses on the right foot, and with a $\pm 4$ bootstrap
spread none of these gaps is individually convincing. We would not present
this as a demonstration that state-space models are the right tool for gait
signals. The two effects that did move the numbers unambiguously were not
architectural at all: how the signal is presented to the network, and how the
data is split.

The failure with raw input made the first point concrete. On folds~1 and~4
training settled at a validation AUC of about $0.53$ while the same code on
folds~2, 3 and~5 passed $0.92$. We first assumed a bug, then an optimisation
problem, and tried longer windows, fresh seeds, warm-up, cosine decay and a
larger encoder; none helped. What helped was replacing the 500 raw samples of a
window by eleven statistics per channel per second. With 60 training subjects a
network reading raw traces must discover both what a gait cycle looks like and
which of its properties separate patients from controls; the statistics supply
the first half, leaving the sequence model to learn how the descriptors evolve.

The bidirectional block was our other architectural idea and it did not survive
its own ablation. A causal scan gives the first tokens of a window almost no
context, which ought to hurt when the whole window is available at inference.
Run at matched size and input, the two variants landed within a thousandth of
each other on both accuracy and AUC. Mean-pooling is the likely explanation: it
already aggregates across the window, so the late tokens of a causal scan can
carry the evidence the classifier needs.

The protocol comparison in Section~\ref{ssec:protocols} has a mechanical
explanation. Windows cut one second apart from the same recording are close to
duplicates, and they share sensor placement, body mass and an individual gait
signature. Split them at random and most test windows have a near neighbour in
the training set, so a classifier can identify the person and recall the label.
The Random Forest makes the size of that shortcut obvious: 73\,\% when subjects
are held out, 98\,\% when only windows are. The SVM-RBF figure under the same
split, 88\,\%, is close to accuracies frequently quoted for this database.

This is also our reading of the gap to the literature. Our results sit in the
70--80\,\% band rather than above 90\,\%, and we do not think that reflects a
weaker method: Figure~\ref{fig:results}(b) reproduces the high numbers from
identical features and identical code.

Two caveats belong here. We searched forty-five configurations, and comparing
them on pooled test accuracy would make the reported number optimistic, which
is why Section~\ref{ssec:selection} selects on validation AUC at a cost of
about three points. And 100 subjects is few: one subject is one accuracy point,
and the $\pm 4$ spread leaves most per-configuration differences insignificant.

Three limitations remain. Only the normal-walk recordings were used, leaving
the dual-task ones unexploited. Stride detection relies on a fixed force
threshold, adequate here but in need of revisiting for severely impaired gait.
And the stride-cycle tokens, which encode exactly the timing variability the
clinical literature emphasises, were the weakest input on their own (66\,\%)
although the same features complement the amplitude statistics in a
subject-level Random Forest -- a shortcoming of our fusion, not of the features.
