\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Key findings.} Three results stand out. First, the Mamba classifier
is the strongest model in this study under the leakage-free protocol: it
improves on the best classical baseline by five accuracy points on both feet
(78\,\% versus 73\,\%) and by twelve points over the previously best deep
baseline (LSTM, 63\,\%). Second, what the model is fed matters more than how
large it is: replacing raw samples by per-second statistics gained seven
points and, more importantly, removed a training collapse that no amount of
capacity or seed averaging had fixed. Third, the evaluation protocol dominates
everything else. The same pipeline reports 78\,\% or above 95\,\% depending
only on whether windows or subjects define the split.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The collapse on folds~1 and~4 with raw input is
informative. With 500 samples per window and 60 training subjects, a model
reading raw force traces has to discover gait structure and discriminative
features simultaneously from few effective examples, and on some folds it
settles into a degenerate solution (validation AUC $\approx 0.53$) while
reaching AUC $> 0.92$ on others. Per-second statistics supply that structure
explicitly, so the sequence model can spend its capacity on how those
descriptors evolve. This is consistent with the assignment's own framing,
which permits either raw signals or feature-vector sequences as input to the
deep models.

The bidirectional ablation, by contrast, did not confirm the corresponding
architectural intuition. A causal scan leaves the first tokens of a window with
little context, which should penalise classification of a fully observed
segment, yet at matched encoder size the causal and bidirectional variants
performed equally (72\,\% each) and the causal one ranked subjects better
(AUC $0.87$ versus $0.82$). Because mean-pooling already aggregates over the
whole window, the late tokens of the causal scan may already carry the
window-level evidence the classifier needs. With 100 subjects, a difference of
this size is also within the bootstrap spread, so the honest reading is that
the experiment is inconclusive rather than that bidirectionality hurts.

The gap between the protocols has a mechanical explanation. Consecutive
one-second windows of the same recording are highly correlated, so a window in
the test set typically has near neighbours in the training set that share
sensor placement, body mass and individual gait signature. The classifier can
identify the subject and recall their label, and the RF result -- 98\,\%
window-wise versus 73\,\% subject-wise -- shows how strong that shortcut is.
Notably, the SVM-RBF baseline reaches 88\,\% under the leaky protocol, close
to values frequently quoted for this database, which suggests such numbers are
often obtained this way.

\textbf{Comparison with prior studies.} Our subject-wise figures sit in the
70--80\,\% band, below the 90\,\%+ commonly reported. We interpret the
difference as protocol rather than method: the leaky columns of
Table~\ref{tab:protocols} reproduce the high numbers with the very same
features and models. Studies that do enforce subject-disjoint evaluation on
this database report values compatible with ours.

\textbf{Strengths and limitations.} Strengths: subject-disjoint splits fixed
in advance, normalisation and hyper-parameter selection confined to training
and validation data, thresholds calibrated on validation subjects only,
seed-averaged results with bootstrap dispersion, and identical evaluation code
for every model. Limitations: 100 subjects is small, so one subject moves
subject-level accuracy by one point and the $\pm 4$ bootstrap spread is
substantial; only the normal-walk recordings were used, leaving the dual-task
recordings unexploited; stride detection uses a simple force threshold that
may misfire on severely impaired gait; and stride-cycle tokens
underperformed on their own (66\,\%), so their clinical information is
probably not yet fully exploited by our fusion.
