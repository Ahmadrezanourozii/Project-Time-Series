\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

Mamba and the Random Forest are statistically tied
(Section~\ref{ssec:baselines}), the expected outcome on a cohort of this size.
Deep sequence models are data-hungry by construction: a Mamba encoder
must learn a representation of gait, and the mapping from it to a diagnosis,
from 60 training subjects per fold, whereas a Random Forest starts from
statistics that already encode what a gait cycle is and has only to weight
them. With 100 subjects a well-tuned classical baseline is not a weak opponent
but the natural favorite, and a state-space model that matches it is doing so
far outside its usual regime.

The two families also differ in what they let a clinician inspect: a Random
Forest exposes feature importances, so a decision traces back to a named
quantity such as the variability of right-foot stance ratio, whereas the Mamba
encoder offers no comparable account of its internal state. Where the two are
statistically tied, that transparency is a real argument for the classical
model.


The raw-input collapse of Section~\ref{ssec:input} makes the same point. With 60 training subjects a network
reading raw traces must discover both what a gait cycle looks like and which of
its properties separate patients from controls; the statistics supply the first
half, leaving the sequence model to learn how the descriptors evolve. The
unstratified sub-studies compound this, since capacity spent on study identity
does not transfer to held-out subjects.

A causal scan gives the first time steps of a window almost no context, yet it
matched the bidirectional block (Section~\ref{ssec:arch}).
Mean-pooling is the likely explanation: it already aggregates
across the window, so the late time steps of a causal scan carry the evidence the
classifier needs.

The protocol effect of Section~\ref{ssec:protocols} has a mechanical
explanation: windows cut one second apart are near-duplicates that share sensor
placement, body mass and an individual gait signature, so under a random split
most test windows have a near neighbor in training and a classifier can
identify the person and recall the label.

Several limitations remain. One subject is
worth one accuracy point, so the $\pm 4$ spread leaves most per-configuration
differences insignificant. Only the normal-walk recordings were used, and
stride detection relies on a fixed force threshold, adequate here but in need
of revisiting for severely impaired gait.
