\section{Experiments \& Results}
\label{sec:exp}

Every experiment uses the five predefined subject-wise folds of
Section~\ref{ssec:data}. Roughly twenty GPU sessions covered the five groups of
experiments below.

\subsection{Experiment 1: what the network is fed}
\label{ssec:input}
Raw signals were not merely weaker but unstable. On folds~1 and~4 the
validation AUC never left the neighborhood of chance ($0.53$ and $0.54$) while
folds~2, 3 and~5 exceeded $0.92$ with the same code and hyper-parameter grid.
Longer windows (30\,s, 60\,s), restarts with fresh seeds and a larger encoder
all failed to repair it. Amplitude-statistic features fixed the problem outright:
the weakest fold rose to $0.75$ validation AUC. Comparing each representation at
its own best configuration, subject-level accuracy on both feet went from
69\,\% with raw 5\,s windows to 80\,\% with amplitude-statistic features, against
65\,\% for raw 60\,s windows and 66\,\% for stride-cycle features (best-case
figures per representation; the configuration actually reported is chosen in
Section~\ref{ssec:selection} and scores 75\,\%). Fusing the stride and
amplitude families did not beat the amplitude features alone, even though the two
are complementary at subject level in a Random Forest (AUC $0.79$, $0.83$ and
$0.86$ for stride, amplitude and both). An unsupervised embedding of the same
features averaged per subject (Figure~\ref{fig:embedding}) shows the two
classes overlapping heavily, with no cluster structure that separates them.

\subsection{Experiment 2: architecture}
\label{ssec:arch}
With the input fixed, encoder capacity was the factor that moved accuracy:
enlarging the encoder from $d_{\text{model}}=128$, $L=4$ to
$d_{\text{model}}=256$, $L=6$ took subject-level accuracy from 72\,\% to 80\,\%
on 30\,s windows. The bidirectional block did not. Run at matched size, matched
input and three seeds each, the causal and bidirectional encoders are
indistinguishable: 77\,\% accuracy and AUC $0.852$ for both. We therefore make
no claim of a benefit from processing the window in both directions, and note
that in the larger encoder size and direction are confounded anyway, since a
bidirectional block doubles the mixer parameters.

Training converged very early: the best epoch was typically between one and four
out of thirty, a symptom of the small effective sample rather than of the
schedule. Stronger regularization (dropout $0.5$) did not help, and neither did
five seeds instead of three or late fusion across feature families.

\subsection{Experiment 3: selecting a configuration without using the test set}
\label{ssec:selection}
Forty-eight configurations were trained in total. Choosing among them by pooled
test accuracy would leak the test set into the model definition, even though
each individual run keeps train, validation and test apart. We therefore ranked
them by mean validation ROC-AUC across the five folds, a quantity that never
sees test data, and report the winner of that criterion.

The two criteria agree on the choice that matters (amplitude-statistic features
win under both) and differ on the details: validation prefers 60\,s windows
with the base encoder, pooled test accuracy 30\,s windows with the large one.
The three accuracy points between them are the optimism a test-based search
would have introduced.

\subsection{Experiment 4: comparison with the baselines}
\label{ssec:baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:main} lists all models under the subject-wise protocol with
identical evaluation code and aggregation. On both feet the Mamba model
leads every baseline, by two accuracy points and $0.03$ AUC over the Random
Forest, but the margin is not uniform: the two tie on the left foot and the
Random Forest is clearly better on the right (78\,\% against 71\,\%).

Two tests keep these numbers in proportion. Since a subject-level decision is
one Bernoulli trial per subject, the last column of Table~\ref{tab:main} tests
each accuracy against the $50$\,\% chance level with a one-sided exact binomial
test: every configuration except the SVM-RBF on the right foot ($p=0.14$) is
significantly above chance, so none of the reported results is a coin flip.
Comparing models with each other is a paired problem, because all of them are
evaluated on the identical 100 subjects; DeLong's test on the ROC curves gives
Mamba $+0.088$ AUC over the SVM-RBF ($p=0.026$), $+0.092$ over the 1D-ResNet
($p=0.006$) and $+0.181$ over the LSTM ($p<0.001$), but only $+0.039$ over the
Random Forest ($p=0.15$). Under an exact McNemar test the accuracy gaps are
smaller than the sample can resolve, except the $+12$ points over the LSTM
($p=0.036$). We therefore read the result as Mamba matching the tuned Random
Forest and beating the other three, not as a clear win over all baselines.

The error profiles differ in a way that matters more than the averages. Mamba
reaches 86\,\% sensitivity at 64\,\% specificity, whereas the Random Forest sits
near 74\,\% on both. For a screening tool the asymmetric profile is preferable:
of the 50 patients it misses seven, at the cost of 18 healthy subjects flagged
for a second look (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}).

\subsection{Experiment 5: the evaluation protocol}
\label{ssec:protocols}
To measure the influence of the split unit we repeated the evaluation with
windows, not subjects, assigned at random to the 60/20/20 splits. Data,
features, models, metrics and code were untouched, and the two classical
baselines were retrained. Accuracy rises by twenty points or more: the Random
Forest goes from 73\,\% to 98\,\% and the SVM-RBF from 68\,\% to 88\,\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}). A per-subject decision has no meaning
here, since a subject's windows are spread across training and test. These
window-level numbers measure how well a model recognizes individuals it has
already seen, not whether it detects Parkinson's disease in a new patient; we
give them purely as a reference point for the literature, where the SVM-RBF
figure of 88\,\% is close to accuracies frequently reported for this database.
