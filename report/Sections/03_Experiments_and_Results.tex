\section{Experiments \& Results}
\label{sec:exp}

Every experiment uses the five predefined subject-wise folds. A model is trained
on the 60 training subjects, its hyper-parameters and decision threshold are
chosen on the 20 validation subjects, and the 20 test subjects are predicted
once. Because the five test sets are disjoint, pooling them gives exactly one
prediction per subject, so every subject is tested once and never appears in two
roles. Roughly twenty GPU sessions covered five groups of experiments: the input
representation, the architecture, the way a configuration is selected, the
comparison against the baselines, and the evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Experiment 1: what the network is fed}
Raw signals were not merely weaker but unstable. On folds~1 and~4 the
validation AUC never left the neighbourhood of chance ($0.53$ and $0.54$) while
folds~2, 3 and~5 exceeded $0.92$ with the same code and hyper-parameter grid.
Longer windows (30\,s, 60\,s), restarts with fresh seeds and a larger encoder
all failed to repair it. Amplitude-statistic tokens fixed the problem outright:
the weakest fold rose to $0.75$ validation AUC. Comparing each representation at
its own best configuration, subject-level accuracy on both feet went from
69\,\% with raw 5\,s windows to 80\,\% with amplitude-statistic tokens, against
65\,\% for raw 60\,s windows and 66\,\% for stride-cycle tokens. (Those are
best-case figures per representation; the configuration actually reported is
chosen in Section~\ref{ssec:selection} and scores 75\,\%.) Stride-cycle tokens
were the weakest input on their own, and fusing them
with the amplitude tokens did not beat the amplitude tokens alone, even though
the two families are complementary at subject level in a Random Forest (AUC
$0.79$, $0.83$ and $0.86$ for stride, amplitude and both).


\subsection{Experiment 2: architecture}
\label{ssec:arch}
With the input fixed, encoder capacity was the factor that moved accuracy:
enlarging the encoder from $d_{\text{model}}=128$, $L=4$ to
$d_{\text{model}}=256$, $L=6$ took subject-level accuracy from 72\,\% to 80\,\%
on 30\,s windows. The bidirectional block did not. Run at matched size, matched
input and three seeds each, the causal and bidirectional encoders are
indistinguishable: 77\,\% accuracy and AUC $0.852$ for both, with the causal
variant marginally ahead at the fixed threshold ($78$ versus $77$\,\%). We
therefore make no claim of a benefit from processing the window in both
directions, and note that in the larger encoder the two factors are confounded
anyway, since a bidirectional block doubles the mixer parameters.

Training converged very early: the best epoch was typically between one and four
out of thirty, a symptom of the small effective sample rather than of the
schedule. We therefore kept dropout at $0.1$--$0.3$, used a three-epoch warm-up
with cosine decay, and averaged three seeds. Stronger regularisation (dropout
$0.5$) did not help, and neither did five seeds instead of three or late fusion
across token families, which stayed below the best single model.

\subsection{Experiment 3: selecting a configuration without using the test set}
\label{ssec:selection}
Forty-eight configurations were trained in total. Choosing among them by pooled
test accuracy would leak the test set into the model definition, even though
each individual run keeps train, validation and test apart. We therefore ranked
all configurations by their mean validation ROC-AUC across the five folds, a
quantity that never sees test data, and report the winner of that criterion.

The two criteria agree on the choice that matters and differ on the details.
Amplitude-statistic tokens win under both. The window length and encoder size
do differ: validation prefers 60\,s windows with the base encoder, pooled test
accuracy prefers 30\,s windows with the large one. The gap between them is
about three accuracy points, which is the optimism a test-based search would
have introduced.

\subsection{Experiment 4: comparison with the baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:main} lists all models under the subject-wise protocol with
identical evaluation code and the same subject-level aggregation, and
Figure~\ref{fig:results}(a) repeats the comparison per foot configuration. On
both feet the Mamba model is ahead of every baseline, by two accuracy points
over the Random Forest and by $0.03$ AUC. The margin is not uniform across foot
configurations: the two models tie on the left foot, and the Random Forest is
clearly better on the right (78\,\% against 71\,\%). Given that a single subject
is worth one accuracy point and the bootstrap spread is $\pm 4$, we would not
read much into any individual one of these gaps.

The error profiles differ in a way that matters more than the averages. Mamba
reaches 86\,\% sensitivity at 64\,\% specificity, whereas the Random Forest sits
near 74\,\% on both. For a screening tool the asymmetric profile is preferable:
of the 50 patients the model misses seven, at the cost of 18 healthy subjects
flagged for a second look. Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the corresponding
confusion matrices.

\subsection{Experiment 5: the evaluation protocol}
\label{ssec:protocols}
To measure the influence of the split unit we repeated the evaluation with
windows -- not subjects -- assigned at random to the 60/20/20 splits. Data,
features, models, metrics and code were left untouched, and the two classical
baselines, which are cheap to fit, were retrained. Accuracy rises by twenty
points or more: the Random Forest goes from 73\,\% to 98\,\% and the SVM-RBF
from 68\,\% to 88\,\%, as Figure~\ref{fig:results}(b) shows. A per-subject decision has no meaning under
this protocol, since a subject's windows are spread across training and test,
so only window-level numbers can be quoted. These numbers measure how well a
model recognises individuals it has already seen, not whether it detects
Parkinson's disease in a new patient, and we give them purely as a reference
point for reading the literature. It is worth noting that the SVM-RBF figure of
88\,\% is close to accuracies frequently reported for this database.
